# Representative Jason Crow: Legislative Agenda Across Three Congresses

**Research brief** | **November 9, 2026** | **117th–119th Congresses (2021–November 9, 2026)**

## Executive assessment

Jason Crow (D–Colorado, 6th District) has pursued a legislative agenda anchored in national security and military oversight, with persistent secondary priorities in veterans’ services, democratic accountability, firearms regulation, small-business opportunity and Colorado-specific investments. Across three Congresses, he has combined legislation he personally introduced with committee amendments, annual defense bills, cosponsorships, and oversight of executive agencies. His committee assignments shape this profile: in the 119th Congress he sits on Armed Services and Intelligence and is ranking member of Armed Services’ Intelligence and Special Operations Subcommittee. [1](#source-1) [2](#source-2)

A key distinction is between agenda and enacted results. Several recurring Crow bills were introduced in more than one Congress without becoming law. His most tangible statutory results have more often been provisions folded into bipartisan defense legislation, support for wider enacted packages, and appropriations for district projects. The agenda below is an analysis of documented activity, not a judgment about policy effectiveness.

## 117th Congress | January 2021–January 2023

### National security, Afghanistan and civilian protection

The U.S. withdrawal from Afghanistan and Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine reinforced Crow’s focus on the consequences of military decisions and intelligence quality. His bipartisan Afghanistan Security Through Intelligence Act (H.R. 5709, 2021) called for a National Intelligence Estimate on Afghanistan. He also pursued more transparency about civilian casualties from U.S. operations, including the Department of Defense Civilian Harm Transparency Act (H.R. 7621, 2022), and proposed making the intelligence community’s climate-security advisory functions permanent. These efforts were primarily proposals and oversight initiatives, not stand-alone enacted laws. [3](#source-3) [4](#source-4)

### Veterans, guns and broad domestic legislation

Crow backed major Democratic and bipartisan measures enacted during the 117th Congress, including infrastructure investment, the CHIPS and Science Act, the Inflation Reduction Act, the PACT Act’s expansion of toxic-exposure benefits, and the Bipartisan Safer Communities Act. His gun-safety stance was also visible in support for the House-passed 2022 assault-weapons ban, which did not become law. The distinction matters: he was a supporter of these broader measures, not their sole author. [6](#source-6) [28](#source-28)

He simultaneously pursued smaller business-development and workforce policies. A bipartisan example is the Small Business Innovation Voucher Act (H.R. 4752, 2021), designed to connect smaller firms with innovation resources. Work on gun-dealer conduct, veterans transitioning to civilian life, federal ethics and government transparency also established proposals that he would reintroduce later. [5](#source-5)

## 118th Congress | 2023–2024

### A minority-party agenda built around oversight and reintroduction

With Republicans controlling the House, Crow’s agenda combined legislative reintroductions and committee scrutiny. He served on the House Foreign Affairs Committee, including as ranking member of its Oversight and Accountability Subcommittee, and continued to work on intelligence and international-security questions. His emphasis was not a wholesale change in policy direction, but an expansion of the mechanisms through which Congress evaluates security decisions and executive-branch conduct. [11](#source-11)

### Foreign affairs, human rights and security institutions

The Civilian Harm Transparency in Partnerships Act (H.R. 6517, 2023), introduced with Rep. Sara Jacobs, would have required annual intelligence reports to Congress on civilian casualties attributable to certain partner-government operations. Crow also introduced legislation concerning intelligence analysis after the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack, Afghanistan reconstruction oversight, State Department overseas crisis management, and modernization of the Bureau of Industry and Security’s information systems. These initiatives stress accountability, lessons learned and administrative capability rather than simply larger defense budgets. [7](#source-7) [11](#source-11)

On military policy, Crow supported the enacted FY2024 and FY2025 National Defense Authorization Acts. His office identified provisions in the FY2025 measure dealing with Buckley Space Force Base, military-family quality of life, civilian-harm assessment, Afghanistan oversight and the State Department’s crisis-response systems. As with all NDAA provisions, the final law—not the earlier House version—is the appropriate benchmark for enacted outcomes. [11](#source-11)

### Firearm regulation, corruption and economic opportunity

Crow sponsored the Firearms Retailer Code of Conduct Act (H.R. 2753), which proposed training requirements for firearms sellers, physical-security measures, liability insurance and notices to deter illegal purchases. He also reintroduced the Closing the Loophole on Interstate Firearm Sales Act (H.R. 1830), aimed at regulating out-of-state long-gun sales in a manner more like handgun transfers. Neither became law. His annual safe-storage resolutions and support for background checks and an assault-weapons ban formed a consistent, mostly non-enacted gun-policy program. [8](#source-8) [26](#source-26) [29](#source-29)

Outside national security, Crow renewed the End Dark Money Act (H.R. 142, 2023) to remove an appropriations restriction limiting IRS action on political disclosures by certain tax-exempt groups. He also promoted small-business workforce pipelines, innovation vouchers, energy loans and land-restoration financing through the Protect the West Act. Many of these bills advanced policy positions or committee discussion without clearing both chambers. [9](#source-9)

### District-specific appropriations

Crow’s office reported that the House passed all 15 of his FY2024 community-project requests, totaling more than $12.2 million, for projects including domestic-violence services, the Aurora Central Library and other local infrastructure or community needs. That March 2024 announcement concerned House passage; it should not by itself be read as proof that each award had already been spent or completed. [10](#source-10)

## 119th Congress | 2025–November 9, 2026

### Continuity in defense; greater emphasis on checks and balances

#### Defense authorization, space and military readiness

Crow returned to the House Armed Services and Intelligence Committees, becoming ranking member for Intelligence and Special Operations. In December 2025, his office credited him with 12 provisions in the final FY2026 NDAA, including military space-domain awareness, rocket-launch modernization, competition in space procurement, civilian-harm reporting, NATO/Russia strategic assessments, Afghanistan War Commission support, and advance notice of major combatant-command restructuring. The NDAA was signed into law as Public Law 119-60. His advocacy has consistently linked Colorado’s Buckley Space Force Base and aerospace sector with broader defense readiness. [1](#source-1) [12](#source-12) [13](#source-13)

Not all defense initiatives have passed. During the FY2027 NDAA committee markup in June 2026, for example, a Crow amendment limiting the assignment of military judge advocates to immigration or other civilian prosecutorial duties failed in committee. This illustrates the difference between a proposed committee amendment and a provision included in an enacted defense law. [25](#source-25)

#### War powers and executive accountability

This Congress brought a sharper focus on constitutional constraints. Crow introduced the No Political Enemies Act (H.R. 7060) in January 2026, proposing protections and remedies against federal enforcement actions motivated by the suppression of protected speech or association. He was an original cosponsor of the bipartisan No Funds for NATO Invasion Act (H.R. 7016), sponsored by Rep. William Keating, to restrict funding for U.S. military action against NATO members or territories. In May 2026, he was an original cosponsor of the No Funds for Iran War Act (H.R. 8707), sponsored by Rep. Pat Ryan, and advocated a war-powers initiative seeking congressional authorization for hostilities involving Iran. Claims about the legality of particular military operations are contested; the clearly documented legislative position is Crow’s effort to condition or prohibit unauthorized hostilities. [17](#source-17) [18](#source-18) [19](#source-19)

#### Veterans, health, migration and economic resilience

In November 2025 Crow introduced a five-bill veterans package addressing suicide at VA facilities, mental-health access, transferable education benefits, assistance to justice-involved veterans and ALS reporting. He also introduced bipartisan legislation on traumatic brain injury and health-sector cyber resilience. These were principally pending proposals as of the reporting date. His 2025 Protect the West Act continued to propose an Outdoor Restoration Fund for resilience and restoration projects. [14](#source-14) [15](#source-15) [16](#source-16)

In 2026 Crow sought temporary protected status for Afghan nationals and an extension for Ukrainians, consistent with earlier advocacy for people affected by U.S. conflicts and allied security crises. He also joined the Improving Medicare Coverage Act (H.R. 10875), introduced by Rep. Pramila Jayapal on October 22, to lower Medicare eligibility to age 50. On October 27 his office announced bipartisan legislation to restrict Russian oil transactions and related sanctions exemptions. [20](#source-20) [21](#source-21) [22](#source-22) [23](#source-23)

## Cross-Congress synthesis | Status and implications

### What remained consistent—and what changed

**Most consistent priority:** defense and intelligence policy, especially military readiness, the conduct of U.S. operations, intelligence/oversight and support for Colorado’s space ecosystem. Crow’s institutional leverage has varied—Foreign Affairs oversight in the 118th Congress, Armed Services and Intelligence leadership in the 119th—but the policy throughline is clear. [1](#source-1) [2](#source-2) [11](#source-11) [12](#source-12)

**Persistent complementary priorities:** veterans’ transition and health, firearm restrictions and safe storage, federal ethics, smaller-business development, climate and land resilience, and transparency in immigration detention. The original law authorizing congressional access to detention facilities dates to 2019 (outside this review period), but his Aurora detention-center monitoring continued throughout 2021–2026. This is a useful example of sustained oversight rather than a newly enacted bill. [24](#source-24) [27](#source-27) [29](#source-29)

**Most visible change:** a move from a broad 117th-Congress legislative coalition under unified Democratic control, to more reintroduction and oversight in the 118th, and then explicit resistance to perceived executive overreach and disputed military actions in the 119th. That shift is visible in his war-powers, NATO, political-retaliation and sanctions proposals, alongside a new emphasis on Medicare affordability. [17](#source-17) [18](#source-18) [19](#source-19) [22](#source-22) [23](#source-23)

### Reading the record correctly

#### Enacted outcomes and active legislative proposals are different

The FY2026 NDAA is enacted law; Crow’s office identifies particular provisions he championed within it. The FY2025 NDAA was likewise enacted. By contrast, the firearm-retailer code, the End Dark Money Act, the Protect the West Act, the No Political Enemies Act and most of the 2025 veterans package were individual bills or resolutions not shown here as enacted. Support for the PACT Act, gun-safety legislation, Medicare expansion or Ukraine aid should be characterized as voting, sponsorship or coalition activity depending on the specific measure. [8](#source-8) [9](#source-9) [13](#source-13) [14](#source-14) [16](#source-16) [17](#source-17)

**Bottom line:** Crow’s legislative agenda is a blend of institutional security oversight and constituent-focused domestic measures. A defensible account of his effectiveness should track three separate outputs: (1) stand-alone laws he principally sponsored, (2) amendments and negotiated provisions adopted in broader bills, and (3) oversight, funding requests and unsuccessful-but-recurrent proposals. Collapsing these categories would overstate—or understate—what the record demonstrates.
