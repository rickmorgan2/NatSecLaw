Title 10 and Title 50

Two titles of the U.S. Code, two oversight systems, and the contested ground between military operations and intelligence activities.

What the labels mean

Each title has its own page: Title 10, Title 50, Title 22, and Title 32.

“Title 10” and “Title 50” are shorthand. Title 10 of the U.S. Code is Armed Forces: how the military is organized, commanded, trained, and used. Title 50 is War and National Defense: it holds the National Security Act, the CIA’s authorities, covert action and intelligence oversight, FISA, and also the War Powers Resolution and the emergency powers statutes.

In practice people use the labels to mean something narrower:

“Title 10” “Title 50”
Kind of activity Military operations Intelligence activities, especially covert action
Typical actor Armed forces under a military commander CIA, or others acting under its authority
Chain of authority President, Secretary of Defense, combatant commander President, Director of the CIA, with the Director of National Intelligence overseeing the community
Approval Orders through the military chain of command Written presidential finding for covert action
Oversight committees Armed Services Intelligence
Deniability U.S. role acknowledged, or at least able to be U.S. role intended not to be acknowledged

The shorthand is imprecise in two ways. The Defense Department holds many Title 50 authorities, since the National Security Agency, the Defense Intelligence Agency, and the other defense intelligence agencies are part of it. And the same people and equipment can operate under either authority depending on who is directing the mission.

The legal hinge: the definition of covert action

The line is drawn by one definition, added to the National Security Act in 1991 and now at 50 U.S.C. 3093(e). Covert action is activity to influence political, economic, or military conditions abroad where the U.S. role is intended not to be apparent or acknowledged. Covert action needs a written presidential finding and notice to the intelligence committees under the rules described on the Intelligence Oversight Act page.

The definition then excludes four categories. The one that matters here is “traditional diplomatic or military activities.” Anything that qualifies as a traditional military activity is not covert action, needs no finding, and is overseen by the armed services committees instead.

The statute does not define traditional military activities. The 1991 conference report described them as activities by military personnel under the direction and control of a U.S. military commander, before or during hostilities that are ongoing or anticipated, where the U.S. role in the overall operation is apparent or will be acknowledged. How far “anticipated” hostilities and “overall operation” stretch is the core of the dispute.

Two terms are often confused. A clandestine operation hides the operation itself. A covert operation hides who is behind it. Military special operations are routinely clandestine without being covert in the statutory sense.

Where the line is contested

Special operations outside war zones. Military preparation of the environment, such as reconnaissance and building local networks, can look identical to intelligence collection. Programs under Section 127e fund surrogate forces for counterterrorism and report to the armed services committees.

Cyber operations. Whether offensive cyber operations against an adversary’s networks in peacetime required a finding was argued for years. Section 394 answered it in 2018 by declaring clandestine military cyber activity a traditional military activity.

Mixed operations. The 2011 raid that killed Osama bin Laden was carried out by military personnel but, by official accounts, conducted under CIA authority. It is the standard illustration that the title describes the authority and chain of command for a mission, not the uniform worn by the people doing it.

Dual roles. One officer has served as both director of the National Security Agency and commander of U.S. Cyber Command, sitting in both systems at once.

Why the distinction matters for oversight

The two committee systems see different things. Covert action comes with a statutory finding and prior notice. Military operations historically came with fewer formal notice rules, on the theory that they are acknowledged and run through an accountable chain of command.

Critics argue this creates an incentive to label sensitive activity as military in order to avoid the finding process. Defenders reply that military operations are subject to the law of armed conflict, the chain of command, and the Uniform Code of Military Justice, and that the intelligence framework was never meant to cover them.

Congress has responded by adding notice rules on the Title 10 side, one activity at a time: sensitive military operations (10 U.S.C. 130f), sensitive military cyber operations (10 U.S.C. 395), and special operations support programs. Most of these arrived through the annual defense authorization. The result is a patchwork that narrows the gap without merging the two systems.

Neighboring titles

The same kind of question, which title and therefore whose rules, comes up elsewhere in this wiki:

  • Title 10 and Title 22. Training and equipping foreign forces can be done by the Defense Department under Chapter 16 of Title 10 or by the State Department under the Foreign Assistance Act and Arms Export Control Act. The choice determines who decides, who pays, and which committees oversee it.
  • Title 10 and Title 32. National Guard members can serve under federal command or under their governor with federal funding. As the mobilization authorities page explains, the status decides whether the Posse Comitatus Act applies.
  • Title 50 and Title 18. Intelligence collection under FISA and criminal investigation run on different standards, and the wall between them has been raised and lowered several times.

Before you rely on this

The law never defines “traditional military activities,” and much of how the government draws the line in practice is classified. So even experts disagree on the details. This page lays out the debate without taking a side; check the linked statute pages and official sources before relying on a specific point.
