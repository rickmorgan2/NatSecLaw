Unit 4: The power of the purse

How does Congress use money to authorize, shape, and stop national security action?

Previous: Unit 3. Deciding to use force | All units | Next: Unit 5. Emergency and economic powers

An authorization says what the government may do; an appropriation decides whether it can pay for it. Two annual acts, Defense and State-foreign operations, fund nearly everything in this wiki, and their general provisions carry standing policy. Fiscal law, chiefly the Antideficiency Act and the Impoundment Control Act, keeps the executive within the amounts and purposes Congress sets. Funding limits have ended a war and restricted a covert program. This unit covers the vocabulary appropriations staff use every day.

What you should be able to do

  • Distinguish an authorization from an appropriation, and explain what happens when only one is enacted.
  • Explain apportionment, transfer, reprogramming, rescission, and deferral.
  • Describe how Congress has used funding limits to end or restrict operations.
  • Explain what the Impoundment Control Act requires and how it has been tested since 2025.

Defense appropriations (annual) 1949 In force Specific authorization

The annual law that provides the Defense Department’s money. The NDAA sets policy and authorizes amounts; this act makes funds available to obligate, account by account, and attaches general provisions that limit how they may be used.

Read it: enacted text

State-foreign ops appropriations 1961 In force Specific authorization

The annual law that funds the State Department and foreign assistance, including the security assistance accounts that pay for Foreign Military Financing, IMET, and Peacekeeping Operations.

Read it: enacted text

Antideficiency Act 1905 In force Framework statute

The fiscal law that makes the purse enforceable. Federal officials may not spend or obligate more than Congress has appropriated, may not commit the government to pay before an appropriation is made, and may not accept voluntary services.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Impoundment Control Act 1974 In force Framework statute

Title X of the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974. It requires the President to spend appropriated funds unless Congress agrees otherwise.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Sec. 8005 transfers 2026 In force Targeted provision

The recurring section of the annual Defense appropriations act that lets the Secretary of Defense, with the approval of the Director of the Office of Management and Budget, move funds between accounts up to a cap set each year ($6 billion for fiscal 2026).

Read it: enacted text

Coup restriction (Sec. 7008) 2026 In force Targeted provision

Bars the use of foreign assistance funds to finance assistance directly to the government of a country whose duly elected head of government is deposed by a military coup or decree, or by a coup or decree in which the military plays a decisive role.

Read it: enacted text

Case-Church Amendment 1973 Lapsed Targeted provision

The funding cutoff that ended U.S. combat in Indochina. Section 307 of the Second Supplemental Appropriations Act, 1973 barred the use of its funds to support, directly or indirectly, combat by U.S. forces in or over Cambodia, Laos, North Vietnam, and South Vietnam or off their shores, and barred any previously appropriated funds for that purpose after August 15, 1973.

Read it: enacted text

Boland Amendments 1982 Lapsed Targeted provision

A series of appropriations limits on U.S. support for the Contras fighting Nicaragua’s government.

Read it: enacted text

Further reading

  • NDAA (annual): The annual law that authorizes funding levels and sets policy for the Defense Department and the nuclear weapons programs of the Energy Department.
  • Constitution (war powers): The Constitution divides national security powers between Congress and the President and leaves the boundary largely undefined.
  • Section 2808 (construction): One of the standby powers unlocked by a national emergency. When a declared war or emergency requires use of the armed forces, the Secretary of Defense may build military construction projects that Congress never authorized, using unspent military construction funds.
  • Section 284 (counterdrug): Authorizes the Defense Department to support the counterdrug and counter transnational organized crime work of other federal agencies and of state, local, and foreign law enforcement, when they ask for it.
  • CIA Act: Gave the CIA the administrative tools to operate in secret: authority to spend money without the usual accounting rules, to withhold information about its staff and structure, and to procure and transfer funds confidentially.

Key terms

Authorization
A law that establishes or continues an agency or program and may recommend funding levels, but does not itself provide money.
Appropriation
Budget authority provided by law to incur obligations and make payments for specified purposes. No money may be drawn from the Treasury without one.
Obligation
A binding commitment, such as a contract or grant, that will require a payment. The Antideficiency Act bars obligations beyond, or in advance of, an appropriation.
Apportionment
The Office of Management and Budget’s division of an appropriation by time period or project, which agencies need before they may obligate funds.
Transfer
Moving budget authority from one appropriation account to another. Allowed only when a statute authorizes it, as Sec. 8005 does for the Defense Department.
Reprogramming
Shifting funds within an account from one purpose to another, governed mainly by notification to the Appropriations Committees rather than by statute.
Rescission
A law cancelling budget authority before it is obligated. The President may propose one under the Impoundment Control Act.
Deferral
A temporary withholding of funds, allowed under the Impoundment Control Act only for limited reasons and not past the end of the fiscal year.
Continuing resolution
A joint resolution that funds agencies, usually at the prior year’s rate, when regular appropriations are not enacted. It generally bars new programs and production increases.

Issues for Congress

Questions that recur in oversight and legislation, with the main arguments on each side. There is no answer key; they are for discussion and briefing preparation.

1. Should Congress rely more on funding limits to check the use of force?

The Case-Church Amendment and the Boland Amendments limited operations through appropriations, and recent bills propose barring funds for military action against specific countries.

  • Supporters argue the purse is Congress’s clearest constitutional check and is harder for the executive to work around than the War Powers Resolution.
  • Critics argue funding cutoffs are blunt, can endanger forces already deployed, and, since they must be enacted like any law, rarely survive a veto.

2. How should Congress respond to impoundments and pocket rescissions?

Since 2025 the Impoundment Control Act has been tested by an enacted rescission of foreign assistance and by a pocket rescission that let funds expire.

  • Some propose tightening the act, for example by barring rescission proposals late in the fiscal year or adding enforcement tools and reporting requirements.
  • Others argue the President needs room to avoid wasteful spending and that the act already provides the right channel, an enacted rescission, for doing so with Congress’s consent.

3. How much transfer and reprogramming flexibility should the Defense Department have?

Sec. 8005 transfers caps general transfers each year and bars using them for items Congress denied.

  • Those favoring more flexibility argue that a budget prepared nearly two years before execution cannot anticipate every need, and that larger transfer authority supports faster response.
  • Those favoring tighter limits argue broad transfer authority weakens Congress’s control over specific programs, and point to the 2019 border barrier transfers as an example.

Check yourself

Try to answer each question before opening it.

Tip1. What is the difference between the NDAA and the Defense appropriations act?

The NDAA (annual) authorizes programs, sets policy, and recommends funding levels. The Defense appropriations (annual) provides the budget authority the Defense Department can actually obligate. An authorized program with no appropriation spends nothing.

Tip2. What does the Antideficiency Act prohibit, and why does it matter in a shutdown?

It bars obligating or spending more than, or before, Congress appropriates, and accepting voluntary services. When appropriations lapse, agencies must stop work except where law allows it, such as emergencies involving the safety of human life or the protection of property. See Antideficiency Act.

Tip3. How did Congress end U.S. combat in Indochina?

With the Case-Church Amendment, a section of a 1973 supplemental appropriations act that barred funds for U.S. combat in or over Cambodia, Laos, North Vietnam, and South Vietnam after August 15, 1973.

Tip4. What happens to funds the President proposes to rescind?

Under the Impoundment Control Act, the President sends Congress a special message and may withhold the funds for 45 days of continuous session; they must then be released unless Congress has enacted a rescission bill. A proposal sent late in the fiscal year, a pocket rescission, can let the funds expire, which GAO has said the act does not permit.
