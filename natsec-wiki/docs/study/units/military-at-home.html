Unit 9: The military at home

When may the armed forces act inside the United States?

Previous: Unit 8. Organizing and commanding the military | All units | Next: Unit 10. Foreign assistance, fragility, and partners

The default rule is that federal troops do not enforce civilian law. The Posse Comitatus Act states the rule; the Insurrection Act is the main exception. Whether the rule applies often turns on the National Guard’s status: under governor control in Title 32 or federalized under Title 10. Counterdrug support and mobilization authorities fill in the rest.

What you should be able to do

  • State the Posse Comitatus rule and its main statutory exception.
  • Explain how Guard duty status determines which rules apply.
  • Describe the tiers of reserve and Guard mobilization authority.

Reading path

Read these in order. Each card links to the wiki page and to the official text. Ticking a box saves your progress in this browser only.

Posse Comitatus Act 1878 In force Framework statute

A one-sentence criminal statute that bars using federal military forces to execute the laws unless the Constitution or an act of Congress expressly authorizes it.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Insurrection Act 1807 In force Framework statute

A set of provisions, first enacted in 1807 and broadened in 1861 and 1871, that lets the President use the armed forces and federalized militia inside the United States to suppress insurrection, enforce federal law, or protect civil rights when ordinary enforcement fails.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Reserve & Guard Mobilization 1952 In force Framework statute

The set of Title 10 provisions, descended from the Armed Forces Reserve Act of 1952 and earlier militia acts, that let the federal government order reserve forces and the National Guard to active duty.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Militia Act of 1903 1903 Superseded Framework statute

Replaced the 1792 militia system. It divided the militia into an organized part, the National Guard, and an unorganized reserve, and gave the Guard federal funding and equipment in exchange for meeting Army standards.

Read it: enacted text, current code

National Defense Act (1916) 1916 Superseded Framework statute

The first comprehensive statute organizing U.S. land forces. It defined the Army as the Regular Army, the National Guard, and a federal reserve, and it let the President draft the Guard into federal service for use overseas.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Section 284 (counterdrug) 1990 In force Targeted provision

Authorizes the Defense Department to support the counterdrug and counter transnational organized crime work of other federal agencies and of state, local, and foreign law enforcement, when they ask for it.

Read it: enacted text, current code

Further reading

  • Militia Acts of 1792: Two acts passed a week apart. The first let the President call out state militias to repel invasion, suppress insurrection, and enforce federal law.
  • Title 32 U.S.C.: The title of the U.S. Code for the National Guard when it is not in federal service. It governs organization, federal funding, and training of the Guard while it remains under the command of state governors.
  • Homeland Security Act: Created the Department of Homeland Security by merging 22 agencies, the largest federal reorganization since 1947.
  • Alien Enemies Act: The only one of the Alien and Sedition Acts still in force. During a declared war, or an invasion or predatory incursion by a foreign nation or government, the President may detain, restrict, and remove nationals of the hostile country.

Key terms

Title 32 status
National Guard duty under the command of a state governor, often with federal funding. Posse Comitatus does not apply.
Federalization
Ordering Guard members into federal service under Title 10, which places them under the President and within Posse Comitatus limits unless an exception applies.

Issues for Congress

Questions that recur in oversight and legislation, with the main arguments on each side. There is no answer key; they are for discussion and briefing preparation.

1. Should the Insurrection Act be narrowed?

The Insurrection Act lets the President use the military domestically on broad and largely undefined conditions.

  • Reform proposals would define the triggering conditions, require consultation with or approval by Congress after a set period, and provide for judicial review.
  • Opponents argue the President needs flexibility in genuine emergencies and that new limits could delay a response when state authorities cannot or will not act.

2. Where should the line fall between federal and state control of the National Guard?

A Guard member’s status under the mobilization authorities decides whether the Posse Comitatus Act applies.

  • Some argue for clearer limits on federalizing a state’s Guard over the governor’s objection.
  • Others argue federal authority must remain available to enforce federal law and protect federal functions.

Check yourself

Try to answer each question before opening it.

Tip1. What does the Posse Comitatus Act prohibit?

Using federal military forces to execute the laws unless the Constitution or an act of Congress expressly authorizes it. See Posse Comitatus Act.

Tip2. What is the principal statutory exception to Posse Comitatus?

The Insurrection Act, which lets the President use the armed forces and federalized militia to suppress insurrection, enforce federal law, or protect civil rights when ordinary enforcement fails.

Tip3. What did the Dick Act do to the old militia system?

It divided the militia into an organized part, the National Guard, and an unorganized reserve, and gave the Guard federal funds and equipment in exchange for meeting Army standards. See Militia Act of 1903.

Tip4. Why does a Guard member’s duty status matter?

Under Title 32 the Guard serves the governor and Posse Comitatus does not apply; federalized under Title 10, it does unless an exception applies. See Reserve & Guard Mobilization and Title 32 U.S.C..
