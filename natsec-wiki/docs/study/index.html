Study guide

How U.S. national security law is layered, and a twelve-unit path through it.

This guide is for anyone learning the national security portfolio, from interns to staff taking on a new committee or issue. It answers two questions: how the laws fit together, and in what order to learn them. Work through the units in order, or go straight to the one you need before a hearing, markup, or meeting. Each unit has a reading path, key terms, Issues for Congress, and questions to check yourself. Progress you tick off is saved in your own browser only.

Is there a hierarchy?

Yes, in three senses, and each part of the site shows one of them.

  1. Authority. Every statute draws on a constitutional power, and many work only through another statute. A sanctions act needs IEEPA, and IEEPA needs a national emergency declared under the National Emergencies Act. The authority stack below sorts every law in the wiki by its place in that chain. The knowledge graph’s Authority layers view draws the same stack with its connections, and each statute page lists what it builds on and what is built upon it.
  2. Codification. Enacted laws are filed by subject into titles of the U.S. Code: Title 10 for the armed forces, Title 50 for war and national defense, Title 22 for foreign relations, Title 32 for the National Guard. The title pages and the Title 10 and Title 50 explainer show this shelf.
  3. Time. Each generation of law answered the failures of the one before: the 1947 system, the 1970s reassertion after Vietnam and Watergate, the post-September 11 expansion, and the recent turn to sanctions, cyber, and civilian harm. The timeline groups the statutes and court decisions by era.

Two things cut across all three. Money: almost every authority here depends on an annual appropriation, and funding limits are among Congress’s strongest tools (Unit 4). The courts: Supreme Court decisions settle what the statutes mean and where presidential power runs out; Justice Jackson’s concurrence in Youngstown is the framework most often used. See the court cases.

Study tools

  • Flashcards. Every statute, court case, and key term, filterable by unit or domain, with spaced review that brings back the cards you miss.
  • Timeline. The statutes and court decisions in order, grouped by era.
  • Court cases. The Supreme Court decisions that define how the statutes work, each linked to the official opinion.
  • Who’s who. The committees, agencies, and courts you will meet, and the laws behind each.
  • Glossary. Every key term from the units in one alphabetical list.
  • Knowledge graph. Switch to Authority layers to see the stack with its connections; click a domain to isolate it.
  • Title 10 and Title 50. The best single page for the military and intelligence divide.
  • Member lenses. How two members of Congress work through these statutes, including on civilian harm.
  • Print. Any unit or statute page prints as a clean packet, with answers expanded and official links written out.

Using this guide in an office

  • Onboarding. Assign a new staffer or intern the units that match the office’s committees. Armed Services: Units 2, 3, 4, 8, 9, and 12. Foreign Affairs or Foreign Relations: Units 3, 5, 10, and 11. Intelligence: Units 6 and 7. Appropriations: Units 4, 10, and 11. Banking (sanctions and export controls): Units 5 and 11.
  • Briefing preparation. Before a hearing or markup, read the “Current status” and “In the courts” sections of the statute pages involved, then the unit’s Issues for Congress, which set out the main arguments on each side without taking one.
  • Trace a chain. Take a current event, such as a strike, a sanctions designation, an arms sale, or a Guard deployment, and trace its legal basis down the stack: which constitutional power, which framework statute, which specific authorization, which provision, and which appropriation pays for it. The graph’s Authority layers view helps.
  • Check yourself. Each unit ends with questions whose answers are hidden. They work for self-study or as a short quiz for an intern cohort.
  • Read the primary text. Every reading card links to the official text. Before quoting a provision in a memo, letter, or bill, confirm it there.
  • Watch the moving parts. Statutes whose status note ends “Check current status” are where the law is changing, and each page shows when it was last checked.
