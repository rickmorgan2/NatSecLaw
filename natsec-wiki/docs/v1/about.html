About this wiki

Scope

The wiki covers the federal statutes that most shape U.S. national security policy, in four core areas, plus a fifth group of foreign policy laws they connect to:

  • War powers and use of force
  • Intelligence and surveillance
  • Foreign aid, fragility, and sanctions
  • Defense organization and arms transfers
  • Related foreign policy legislation

A sixth group, constitutional and code foundations, holds what the rest is built on: the Constitution’s war and foreign affairs clauses, the founding-era statutes, the laws that created the National Guard, and overview pages for Titles 10, 22, 32, and 50 of the U.S. Code. Each title page lists every statute in the wiki codified in that title.

A few entries are single U.S. Code provisions, such as 10 U.S.C. 127e, where the provision matters more than the act that carried it. The Title 10 and Title 50 page explains the divide between military and intelligence authorities and lists the statutes in each title.

Apart from the Constitution page, it covers statutes only. Treaties, executive orders, court decisions, and regulations appear where they explain a statute, but they do not have their own pages.

Member lens

This copy of the wiki is tailored to one member of Congress. The Member lens page maps that member’s committee seats, legislative record, and district interests onto the statutes. It is an independent reference built from public sources, it is not affiliated with or endorsed by the member’s office, and it describes the record without taking a position on it.

Reading a statute page

Each page has the same parts: a citation bar with the law’s status, a short summary, key provisions, why the law matters, its current status, its connections to other statutes, and source links.

Links between statutes in the text and in the Connections table are the wiki’s cross-references. The knowledge graph draws the same connections.

Relationship types

Connections are directional and use a fixed vocabulary.

Relationship Meaning
amends The first statute changed the text of the second.
repeals The first statute repealed the second, in whole or in part.
succeeds The first statute took over the role of the second.
relies on The first statute operates through authority or machinery in the second.
constrains The first statute limits how the second may be used.
enacted in The first statute was passed as part of the second, usually a defense authorization.
related to A thematic or historical link without a direct legal dependency.

Status labels

Status Meaning
In force The statute is current law. It may have been amended.
Partly expired Core provisions are current, but significant authorities have lapsed or depend on short extensions.
Lapsed The central authority expired and had not been renewed at last review.
Repealed Congress repealed the statute.
Superseded A later statute replaced it.

Accuracy and limits

The summaries were drafted with Claude, an AI model, and reviewed on November 10, 2026. Items that changed in 2025 and 2026 were checked against current reporting, and those sources are listed on the relevant pages. Citations and section numbers were written from reference knowledge and have not each been checked against the primary text.

Treat the wiki as a map for finding and relating statutes. Before citing a provision in your own work, confirm it in the primary source linked at the bottom of each page. Statutes with sunset clauses change fastest; the pages for Section 702, the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act, the Defense Production Act, the Iran Sanctions Act, and the annual defense authorization all carry a note to check current status.

Primary links are built from citations using fixed URL patterns. The GovInfo link service covers public laws from the 104th Congress (1995) onward and the Statutes at Large from volume 65 (1951) onward, so older laws link to the U.S. Code instead.

This is a reference tool and not legal advice.

Adding or changing content

All content lives in data/. The pages in statutes/ are generated and should not be edited by hand.

  1. Add or edit an entry in one of the files in data/statutes/. Copy an existing entry to get the fields right.
  2. Add rows to data/edges.csv for its connections, using the relationship types above.
  3. Run quarto preview. The build script checks the data and stops with a message if an ID, domain, status, or relationship is not recognized.

Each statute’s U.S. Code titles are read from its usc citation. To add a title that the citation does not show, list it under usc_titles, for example usc_titles: [10].

To link to another statute inside a summary, write its ID in double square brackets, for example [[fisa-1978]].
